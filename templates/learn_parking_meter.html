Parking meter QR scams: check the code before you pay

A QR code on a parking meter may be part of a legitimate payment flow. It may also be a sticker placed over an operator's real code. The FTC has received reports of this substitution, and the FBI warns that a replaced code can send a payment or login to a criminal's site.[1][2]

The printed sign is not proof of the link's owner. Use this check before you pay.

1. Look at the code and its surroundings

Check whether the QR is printed as part of the meter's sign or appears to be a separate layer. A sticker edge, a code pasted over another code, or a label that does not match the surrounding instructions deserves a pause. The FBI specifically calls out stickers placed over an original QR code as a tampering sign.[2]

Do not treat appearance as a final verdict. A neat sticker can still be fraudulent, and some legitimate operators may use stickers. Verify the payment method independently.

2. Check the operator's own rules

Toronto's Green P illustrates why a generic “QR means scam” rule fails. Its pay-by-plate meters offer a legitimate scan-to-pay option. But Green P's May 2026 alert says its older machines have no QR capability and its newer machines show the code on the screen: a separate QR sticker on either type is fraudulent. That is Green P's rule, not a rule for every parking company. Check the operator's current official instructions for the meter in front of you.[4][5]

3. Find the operator without using the QR link

Read the operator name and parking zone from the meter or nearby official sign, where available. Open the operator's known app or type its known website yourself. If you are unsure, use the posted kiosk or an independently verified customer-service number. Compare the location, zone, and amount in the payment flow with the meter before entering card details. The FBI recommends using known, trusted addresses instead of paying through a QR destination.[2]

If you cannot establish which company runs the meter or whether it accepts QR payment, pause and ask the parking operator. A demand to pay only by scanning a code is not enough evidence that the code is official.

Before submitting payment in a verified app or website, check that the displayed location, zone or space, vehicle plate, parking time, and amount match what you intend to buy, where the operator shows those details. After payment, check the session or receipt in that same official channel. A confirmation shown only on an unverified QR page does not establish that the operator received your payment. If the details disagree, stop and contact the operator instead of trying another QR link.

4. Read the destination before opening it

Many phone QR readers show a link preview. Read the website address and watch for misspellings or switched letters. A logo or the word “parking” on a page does not verify who operates the domain. If the preview is shortened, unfamiliar, or does not match the operator you independently found, use the known app, website, or kiosk instead.[1][2]

Do not rely on a padlock or “https” alone. The FBI has documented phishing pages that use HTTPS and says the lock icon does not establish that a site is trustworthy. The question is whether the address belongs to the operator you found independently, not just whether the connection is encrypted.[6]

You can inspect an unopened QR code with Sqafe, but a scan result reflects only the code's encoded content and available signals at that moment. It cannot tell whether the meter's sticker was authorized or whether the payment recipient is the parking operator. Independent verification still matters.

If the QR is the only visible option

Do not treat a suspicious code as proof that no payment is due or that a parking session has started. Look for an official alternative on the machine or nearby instructions, use the operator's independently found app or website if available, or contact the operator through a known channel. If you cannot verify any payment method, choose another parking location rather than entering details into a page you do not trust. Tell the operator the exact meter location so it can inspect the code.

If you already paid or entered details

Stop using the suspicious page. Contact your card issuer or bank through its official app or the number on your card. Explain what happened and ask it to protect the account and review or reverse a charge. Review your transactions. If you entered a password, change it on the real service and on any other account where you reused it, then enable two-factor authentication.[3][1]

Tell the parking operator which meter or zone displayed the code so it can inspect the sign. In the United States, report suspected fraud at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and IC3.gov.[1][2] For other steps after a suspicious scan, read what to do after scanning a suspicious QR code.
