QR safety guide

Scanned a suspicious QR code? What to do next

Take a breath and work from what happened after the scan. A QR code can lead to a website, an app download, or a payment flow. A tampered code can send you somewhere other than the place shown on a sign or message.[1] Stop interacting with the page or app while you check which steps apply.

You saw a preview or opened a page, but entered nothing

Close the page. Do not use a phone number, chat widget, or “help” link on that page to investigate it. If the code claimed to come from a business, open the business's known website or app yourself, or contact it through a number you independently trust.[2][1]

Check that your phone and apps are up to date. The FTC recommends updates to reduce the risk from harmful links. Seeing a page does not, by itself, tell you whether your phone was compromised. If the page asked you to download something, grant a permission, sign in, or pay, use the applicable section below if you did any of those things.[2]

If you allowed a browser or app permission while visiting the page, review that permission in your device settings and remove access you do not recognize. Write down which access you allowed before seeking help. The FBI advises caution before authorizing phone permissions from an unsolicited QR destination and recommends including granted permissions in an incident report.[8]

You entered a password or verification code

Go to the real service directly, using its saved app or an address you type yourself. Change the affected password immediately and change it on other accounts where you reused it. Turn on two-factor authentication. If you cannot sign in, use the real service's account-recovery process.[3]

If you shared a one-time verification code, contact the real account provider through its official support channel and review recent account activity. The FTC warns that a scammer who obtains a verification code may be able to sign in to an account.[4] If the affected account belongs to your employer or school, tell its security or IT team promptly.

You entered card or bank details, or made a payment

Contact your card issuer or bank now, using the number on your card or its official app. Tell them what you entered or paid, ask them to protect the account, and ask whether a payment can be reversed. Check your transactions for charges you do not recognize. For a payment app or bank transfer, report the transaction through that service immediately.[3][2]

If you also entered a password, follow the password steps above. In the United States, if you gave other sensitive personal information, the FTC's identity-theft recovery guide provides steps based on what was exposed.[3]

You sent cryptocurrency

If the QR destination led you to a cryptocurrency payment and you sent funds, contact the exchange or ATM operator immediately. Explain that the transaction was fraudulent and ask whether it can be reversed. The FTC cautions that cryptocurrency payments do not have the same protections as card payments and can be difficult to recover.[3] Keep the transaction ID and destination address for an IC3 report, and be wary of anyone promising to recover the funds.[9]

You installed an app or gave device access

Stop using the app and do not sign in to sensitive accounts from a device you suspect is compromised. Remove an app you do not trust, update the device, and run the security checks available for your device. If you need help assessing or removing malware, contact the device maker or a support provider you already know and trust. Use a device you trust to change important passwords and enable two-factor authentication promptly.[5][6][7]

The FBI advises downloading apps through your phone's app store instead of through a QR-code destination.[1]

Report the code and keep useful details

If the QR code was on a parking meter, menu, poster, or other physical surface, tell the location's operator where you found it. Keep the URL, a screenshot of the page, and a photo of the code or sign if you can do so safely. Do not put passwords or full card numbers in a public report. For parking-specific checks, read our parking meter QR scam guide.

In the United States, report suspected fraud to ReportFraud.ftc.gov and, for internet crime, IC3.gov. Report a payment problem to the payment provider first. Reporting does not guarantee that money will be recovered.[3][1]

For the next QR code, inspect it with Sqafe's scanner before opening its destination, and verify a business or payment request independently. A scan result reflects available signals at that moment. It cannot certify that a physical sign, business, or payment recipient is genuine.
